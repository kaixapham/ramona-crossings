(03) The slow way

We still cross the bay the slow way, by sail.

No diesel. No hurry.

In a harbour of outboards and water taxis, we choose the long way round. Two hulls, one big blue sail and a crossing that takes as long as the wind says, which is about ninety minutes, there and back.

A good crossing is mostly patience, and a little wind.

(Wind) North-west, most evenings, enough to fill the gennaker. It eases at sunset and the bay goes flat as a plate.

(Speed) Six to eight knots under sail. Two slim hulls leave a wake small enough to rock the moored dinghies, not to swamp them.

(Seats) Twenty-four, in the cockpit, on the flybridge and in the bow lounge. Nobody stands, and nobody is more than three metres from the rail.

A small crew, a few regulars, and whoever the evening brings aboard.

Skipper, first mate, a deckhand who knows every buoy by name, and a kettle that has never been allowed to go cold.

Leaveonly awake.

She sails most of the way and slips in and out of the harbour on two electric motors. Solar panels on the flybridge roof charge her batteries, the propellers top them up while she sails, the bilge is pumped ashore, and the only thing she leaves on the bay is a V of white water that closes behind her in a minute.
